## The short answer

Bowlers break down when their **workload changes faster than their body can adapt**. A 2014 study of 28 elite fast bowlers found that when a bowler's workload in one week was more than double their average over the previous four weeks, their risk of injury the following week was several times higher.

## What the study did

Researchers followed 28 elite fast bowlers over six years — 43 individual seasons. For each week they compared:

- **Acute workload:** what the bowler did that week, measured as balls bowled and as a session-effort score.
- **Chronic workload:** the rolling average over the previous four weeks — roughly, what the bowler's body was used to.

## What they found

- When acute load was **more than twice** chronic load, injury risk in the following week was about **3–4.5 times** higher than in weeks where the two were balanced.
- A **negative** balance — doing much less than usual — was also associated with higher risk, roughly double.

The authors concluded that large, sudden increases in workload are associated with injury.

## Why the debate

The idea spread quickly into the acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR) used across many sports. Later statisticians argued that the ratio has built-in mathematical problems — for example, the acute week is part of the chronic average — and that using it as a single number to make decisions about individuals can mislead. Most practitioners now treat workload as one input alongside how the bowler feels, their history and their technique.

## On the field

- **Build up gradually.** Returning from a break, bowlers increase their overs step by step rather than jumping straight to match loads.
- **Don't drop off completely.** Long gaps followed by heavy bowling are risky, which is why bowlers keep a base load between series.

## Common misconceptions

- *"Bowling a lot causes injury."* High workloads that a bowler has built up to are associated with lower risk. Sudden **changes** are the problem.
- *"There is a safe ratio."* No single number reliably separates safe from unsafe for an individual.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: relative risk</summary>

The study reports relative risk — how many times more likely an injury was in one situation compared with another. A relative risk of 4 does not mean a 4% or 40% chance of injury; it means four times the baseline rate, whatever that baseline was. Absolute risk for any individual week remained modest.
