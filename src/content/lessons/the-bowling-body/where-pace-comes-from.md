## The short answer

Pace does not come from a strong arm. It comes from **sequencing**. The run-up builds momentum, the front leg plants and stops the lower body, and that energy is passed up through the hips, trunk, shoulder, arm and wrist — each segment speeding up just as the one before it slows down. Like cracking a whip, the tip ends up moving much faster than the handle.

## The phases

1. **Run-up.** Builds controlled momentum. Rhythm and repeatability matter more than top speed.
2. **Bound (pre-delivery stride).** The last leap that sets up the body's position for delivery.
3. **Back-foot contact.** The back foot lands; the hips and shoulders are set, ready to rotate.
4. **Front-foot contact.** The front foot lands and the leg braces. Ground reaction forces at this moment are several times body weight.
5. **Release and follow-through.** The arm comes over, the wrist flicks the ball out, and the follow-through spreads the remaining energy safely.

The phases are a real sequence: each one sets up the next.

## The front leg

A study of 20 elite fast bowlers found that the fastest kept a **straighter front knee** through front-foot contact. Together with a quicker run-up, more upper-trunk flexion and a later start to the arm's circling motion, it explained most of the difference in release speed. A braced leg works like a pole vaulter planting a pole: it stops the lower body and turns forward momentum into rotation of the trunk and arm.

A braced leg also means high forces through the knee and lower back. A 2016 study of 20 elite fast bowlers found that faster bowlers did not have higher peak vertical forces; what went with speed was a larger braking impulse from the front leg between landing and release. Speed and load need not be a trade-off.

## On the field

- **Run-up speed is not the same as pace.** A rhythmic approach that a bowler can repeat beats a sprint they cannot control.
- **Losses add up.** A collapsing front knee, an early-opening chest or a stalled trunk each leak energy before it reaches the hand.

## Common misconceptions

- *"Fast bowlers are fast because they are strong."* Strength helps, but sequencing and timing make the biggest difference.
- *"Every bowler should have a dead-straight front leg."* The association is strong, but bowlers have reached high speeds with different patterns. Changes to a junior's action should be made with a qualified coach.

Biomechanists call this proximal-to-distal sequencing. Heavy, slow segments near the body's centre start moving first; lighter segments further out reach higher speeds later. Each segment's peak angular velocity occurs slightly after the one before it. When the timing is right, energy is passed along rather than wasted.
