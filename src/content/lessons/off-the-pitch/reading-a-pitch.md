## The short answer

A pitch is a strip of rolled clay soil with a thin layer of grass. How much water is in it, how much grass is left on it and how hard it has been rolled decide how the ball behaves after it lands. Early on, moisture and grass help seamers. Later, the surface dries, cracks and wears, which helps spinners and makes bounce less predictable.

## What each factor does

- **Moisture.** A damp surface is softer and grips the seam. When the ball lands on its seam, the seam digs in slightly and the ball can deflect sideways — **seam movement**. Moisture also tends to slow the ball and keep bounce lower.
- **Live grass.** Grass protects the surface and holds moisture. A green pitch usually means more help for seam bowlers, especially in the first session.
- **Hardness.** A well-rolled, dry, clay-rich pitch is hard. Hard surfaces return more of the ball's energy, so it comes through quicker and bounces higher.
- **Cracks.** As the pitch dries it shrinks and cracks. A ball landing on the edge of a crack can jump, keep low or change direction.
- **Footmarks.** Bowlers' follow-throughs scuff patches outside the line of the stumps. Spinners aim at them because the roughened surface grips and turns the ball.

## How a pitch changes over a Test

- **Day 1:** freshest surface, most moisture — usually the best time for seamers.
- **Days 2–3:** often the best batting conditions, as the surface firms up but has not yet broken down.
- **Days 4–5:** drier, cracked and worn — more turn for spinners and more variable bounce.

This is a common pattern, not a rule. Climate, soil type and preparation can change it completely.

## Common misconceptions

- *"A green pitch is always fast."* Grass often means moisture, and moisture tends to slow the ball. Pace comes from hardness.
- *"You can read a pitch by looking at it."* Research on county pitches found that a simple hardness test predicted bounce about as well as umpires' judgement — and that umpires' ratings of pace were unreliable.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: measuring a pitch</summary>

Researchers measure pitch hardness with a **Clegg hammer**: a weight dropped onto the surface while a sensor records how sharply it decelerates. A harder surface stops it more abruptly. In a study of 92 English county pitches, Clegg hammer readings predicted the bounce umpires reported, and a model using bounce and surface friction predicted the ball's pace after bouncing.
