## The short answer

After sunset, the outfield cools quickly and water vapour in the air condenses on the grass as **dew**. Every time the ball runs across the outfield it picks up moisture. A wet ball is slippery to hold and tends to skid off the pitch. That makes bowling — especially spin — harder in the second half of an evening match.

## How it works

Dew forms when a surface cools below the **dew point**, the temperature at which air becomes saturated. On clear, still evenings, grass radiates heat to the sky and cools faster than the air above it, so dew can be heavy. Humid regions and seasons produce more.

A wet ball affects the game in two ways:

- **Grip.** Spin and variations like cutters depend on the fingers gripping the seam and leather. Moisture reduces that grip.
- **Bounce.** A wet surface reduces friction at the bounce, so the ball skids on instead of gripping and turning.

## On the field

- **The toss.** In evening matches where heavy dew is expected, captains often bowl first so their own bowlers have the dry ball and the opposition bowls with the wet one.
- **Drying the ball.** Fielders and umpires use towels to dry the ball between deliveries. Law 41.3 allows a fielder to dry a wet ball on a cloth approved by the umpires; most other changes to the ball are unfair play.
- **Ground management.** Some venues spray chemical anti-dew treatments on the outfield before evening matches.

## Common misconceptions

- *"Dew is rain."* Dew condenses from the air already there; it does not fall.
- *"Dew helps seamers swing it."* A wet, heavy ball tends to make swing and grip harder, not easier.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: why still, clear nights bring the most dew</summary>

Clouds act like a blanket, sending heat back down to the ground. On a clear night, the grass radiates heat straight to space and cools fast. Wind mixes warmer air down to the surface and slows the cooling. So clear skies, little wind and humid air give the heaviest dew.
