## The short answer

At the bounce, the ball is squashed against the pitch for a few thousandths of a second. Some of its energy goes into deforming the ball and the surface and is lost. A **hard** pitch loses less, so the ball bounces higher and keeps more speed. **Friction** decides how much forward speed is stripped away as the ball skids and grips.

## How it works

Think of the bounce in two directions:

- **Vertical:** the ball arrives moving downwards and leaves moving upwards. The ratio of those two speeds is the vertical *coefficient of restitution*. Harder, drier, well-rolled surfaces have a higher value, so the ball rebounds more steeply.
- **Horizontal:** the ball slides along the surface at first. Friction slows that sliding — and, on a spinning ball, can grip and change its direction. More friction means more speed lost along the pitch.

A ball that keeps its speed and climbs steeply after landing reaches the keeper higher and harder. That is what players feel as a "quick" pitch.

## On the field

- **Bounce and carry.** On a hard pitch, edges carry to the slips. On a soft one, they can fall short.
- **Variable bounce is the batter's real enemy.** A consistent surface, fast or slow, can be learned. One that keeps low on some deliveries and climbs on others cannot.
- **Back of a length.** Bowlers who hit the pitch hard from a height get the most from a hard surface, because the steeper the arrival angle, the steeper the rebound.

## Common misconceptions

- *"Grass makes a pitch fast."* Grass often holds moisture, which softens the surface. Hardness gives pace.
- *"Pace is easy to judge by eye."* In a study of county pitches, umpires' pace ratings did not match measured pace — they followed bounce instead.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: modelling pace</summary>

In the Sheffield Hallam study, a model that combined measured bounce and surface friction predicted the ball's speed after the bounce, and agreed reasonably well with high-speed video of real deliveries. Hardness alone was enough to predict bounce; pace needed friction as well.
