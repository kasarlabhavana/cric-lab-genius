## The short answer

Ask any swing bowler and they will tell you the ball moves more under heavy cloud. That observation is widespread and deserves to be taken seriously. But the **reason** is not settled. Wind-tunnel studies have not found a clear way in which humidity itself changes the airflow enough to increase swing.

## What we know

Swing is controlled by the boundary layer: whether it is laminar or turbulent on each side of the ball and where it separates. The main levers are ball speed, seam angle, seam prominence and surface condition. Those are well established.

Humid air is very slightly *less* dense than dry air, and its viscosity barely changes over the range seen at cricket grounds. Neither change is large enough, on its own, to explain a dramatic increase in swing.

## The candidate explanations

- **Calmer air.** On a sunny day, heat rising from the ground stirs the air. Under cloud the air near the ground may be calmer, which could help the shiny side stay laminar. Plausible, not proven.
- **A slightly damp surface.** Moisture could change how the lacquer and leather behave, or swell the seam. Plausible, not proven.
- **What is happening off the pitch.** Overcast days often come with a fresher pitch and a newer ball, both of which help seam bowlers. Some of the "swing" credited to cloud may be seam movement or simply better bowling conditions overall.
- **Memory.** Balls that swing under cloud are memorable; balls that do not are forgotten. That does not mean the effect is imaginary, but it means anecdote alone cannot measure it.

## How to think about it

Treat "cloud helps swing" as a strong field observation with an open explanation. A good test would measure swing on many deliveries across different weather while controlling ball age, speed and seam position. Until that is done carefully, anyone who states the cause with certainty is going beyond the evidence.
