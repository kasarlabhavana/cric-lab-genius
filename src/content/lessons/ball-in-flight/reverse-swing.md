## The short answer

When a ball is old and rough, the air on **both** sides turns turbulent. The seam stops helping its side hold on to the air and starts doing the opposite: it thickens the boundary layer on that side so it separates earlier. The force flips, and the ball moves away from where the seam points.

## How it works

In conventional swing, one side is laminar and one is turbulent. That asymmetry pushes the ball towards the seam's direction.

As the ball gets faster, or rougher, the smooth side eventually turns turbulent too. Once both sides are turbulent, the seam is no longer a trip — it is an obstacle. It acts like a ramp, pushing air away from the surface and leaving the boundary layer on that side thicker and weaker. That side now separates **first**. The wake swings round, and the ball is pushed away from the seam's direction.

Roughness is the key ingredient. A rough surface turns the boundary layer turbulent at lower speed. Wind-tunnel tests reported by Rabindra Mehta found that reverse swing needed around 180 km/h with a new two-piece ball, around 130 km/h with a new four-piece ball, and only around 110 km/h with a roughened old ball.

## On the field

- **Ball management.** Teams keep one side as smooth as they legally can and let the other side wear. The worn side helps turbulence form; the smooth side gives the contrast bowlers want.
- **Dry, abrasive pitches** scuff the ball faster, which is why reverse swing has a long history in hot, dry conditions.
- **Same grip, opposite movement.** A bowler can hold the ball as if for an outswinger and get it to move in. For the batter, the usual visual cue from the seam points the wrong way.

## Common misconceptions

- *"Reverse swing means the shiny side and rough side have swapped jobs."* The real change is in the flow regime — both sides turbulent — not just in which side is polished.
- *"Reverse swing needs ball tampering."* Altering the ball illegally can produce it, but a ball worn naturally on a dry square can reverse legally.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: the Reynolds number</summary>

Whether a boundary layer is laminar or turbulent depends on the Reynolds number, Re = v·D/ν (speed × diameter ÷ the air's kinematic viscosity). A 7.2 cm ball at 30 m/s (108 km/h) has Re ≈ 140,000. On a smooth sphere, transition to turbulence happens around Re ≈ 200,000. Roughness and the seam bring that threshold down. Reverse swing is what happens on the far side of that transition.

</details>
