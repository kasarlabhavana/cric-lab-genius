## The short answer

A spinning ball carries a layer of air round with it. On one side the ball's surface moves with the oncoming air; on the other it moves against it. That shifts where the airflow leaves the ball, deflecting the wake one way and pushing the ball the other. This is the **Magnus force**. Side-spin makes the ball **drift**; top-spin makes it **dip**.

## How it works

The Magnus force always acts at right angles to the direction the ball is travelling, and its direction is set by the spin axis.

- **Top-spin** (the ball rolling forward) pushes the ball down. It drops faster than gravity alone would make it — so it lands shorter than the batter expects.
- **Side-spin** pushes it sideways. Wrist-spinners' stock leg-breaks are often seen to drift **into** a right-handed batter before turning **away** off the pitch. How much drift appears depends on how the spin axis is tilted at release.
- **Back-spin** pushes it up, so the ball "floats" and lands fuller.

The force grows with spin rate, airspeed and air density. Spinners bowl slowly compared with seamers, so they rely on high revs. Broadcast ball-tracking now measures spin rate directly, and elite wrist-spinners regularly register well over 2,000 revolutions per minute.

## On the field

- **Dip beats the batter's judgement of length.** A ball that dips late lands shorter than its early flight suggested, which can draw a batter forward into a false stroke.
- **Drift beats their judgement of line.** A ball that drifts in, then turns away, makes the batter play down the wrong line twice.
- **Most spin deliveries mix top-spin and side-spin.** Changing the mix — wrist position at release — is how a spinner varies dip and drift without changing the action much.

## Common misconceptions

- *"The Magnus force causes swing."* Conventional and reverse swing come from the seam and surface changing the boundary layer, not from spin. Swing bowlers release the ball with very little sideways spin.
- *"Turn and drift are the same."* Turn happens when the ball grips the pitch. Drift happens before it gets there.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: why a cricket ball's Magnus effect is modest</summary>

The Magnus force scales with the ratio of the ball's surface speed (from spin) to its travel speed. A spinner bowling at 80–90 km/h with high revs has a large ratio, so drift and dip are visible. A fast bowler at 140 km/h with only modest spin has a small ratio — which is one reason fast bowlers use the seam, not spin, to move the ball in the air.
