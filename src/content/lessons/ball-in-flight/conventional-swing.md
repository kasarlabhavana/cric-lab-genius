## The short answer

A new ball swings because the air flows differently over its two halves. The seam, held at an angle, trips the air on one side into turbulence. Turbulent air clings to the ball further round than smooth air does. The wake behind the ball is pushed to one side, and the ball is pushed the other way — in the direction the seam points.

## How it works

Air touching the ball forms a thin **boundary layer**. On a smooth surface at moderate speed this layer is **laminar**: orderly, but low on energy, so it peels away from the ball early — roughly at the ball's widest point.

When the seam is angled, say towards the slips, the air reaching that side meets the raised stitching. The seam acts as a trip and turns the boundary layer **turbulent**. A turbulent layer mixes faster air down towards the surface, so it stays attached further round the back of the ball before separating.

Now the two sides are different. One side lets go early; the other holds on. The wake leaving the ball is deflected towards the early-separating side, and by Newton's third law the ball is pushed towards the seam side. That sideways push is swing.

## On the field

- **Seam position matters more than the action.** Bowlers aim to release the ball with the seam upright and tilted a little — wind-tunnel work suggests around 20° is effective.
- **Shine matters.** Keeping one side smooth helps it stay laminar. Since 2020, international cricket has banned saliva for shining, so bowlers use sweat.
- **Speed has a window.** Too slow and the seam cannot trip the flow properly. Too fast and the smooth side turns turbulent on its own, and the difference between the sides disappears.

## Common misconceptions

- *"The shiny side makes the ball move towards it."* For conventional swing the ball moves towards the seam's direction, which on most deliveries means away from the shiny side.
- *"Swing is caused by spin."* Swing bowlers deliberately put little sideways spin on the ball. The force comes from the seam and surface, not from rotation. Spin-driven curve is a different effect — the Magnus force.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: why a turbulent layer holds on longer</summary>

Behind the ball's widest point, air pressure rises again. Air in the boundary layer has to push into that rising pressure. A laminar layer lacks the momentum near the surface to do it for long, so it separates. A turbulent layer constantly mixes faster outer air into the slow layer near the surface, giving it the momentum to keep going. Golf balls are dimpled for the same reason: turbulence delays separation and shrinks the wake.
