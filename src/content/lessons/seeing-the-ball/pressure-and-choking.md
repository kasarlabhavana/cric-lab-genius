## The short answer

A well-learned skill — playing a forward defence, bowling a yorker — normally runs **automatically**. Under pressure, players can start paying close attention to how they are moving and trying to control it step by step. That conscious control can break the smooth, automatic version of the skill. This is one main explanation for **choking**.

## Two explanations

- **Explicit monitoring.** Pressure makes performers focus on their own movements. Research by Sian Beilock and Thomas Carr found that experienced golfers putted worse when pressure pushed them to attend to their technique, while novices — who were still thinking about technique anyway — were less affected.
- **Distraction.** Pressure fills the mind with worry about the result, which takes up the working memory needed for the task. This matters most for tasks that need thinking — like setting a field or planning an over.

A related idea, **reinvestment**, suggests that players who learned a skill through explicit rules ("elbow up, head over the ball") have more rules to fall back on — and fall back on them under pressure.

## On the field

- **Pre-delivery routines.** A consistent routine between balls gives the mind something familiar to do and reduces over-thinking.
- **External focus.** Thinking about the target or the ball's path, rather than body parts, tends to keep skills automatic.
- **Training under pressure.** Practising with consequences — scores, targets, small stakes — helps players get used to the feeling.

## Common misconceptions

- *"Choking means a player is mentally weak."* It is a predictable effect of pressure on how attention is used, and it affects skilled players precisely because their skills are automatic.
- *"Just concentrate harder."* Concentrating on the wrong thing — your own movements — can make it worse.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: implicit learning</summary>

Some research suggests that skills learned with fewer explicit instructions — for example, by learning through varied practice and feedback rather than step-by-step rules — hold up better under pressure. The evidence is promising but mixed, and most coaching combines both approaches.
