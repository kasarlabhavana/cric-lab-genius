## The short answer

Expert batters start reading a delivery **before the ball leaves the bowler's hand**. The position of the wrist, fingers and arm gives away clues about type and length. Studies show highly skilled batters use those early cues; less skilled batters mostly wait for the ball's flight.

## How the research works

The standard method is **temporal occlusion**. Batters watch video of a bowler, and the footage cuts off at different moments — before release, at release, or partway through the flight. They then predict the delivery's type and length. If accuracy is better than chance even when the clip stops before release, the batter must be using pre-release information.

A related method, **spatial occlusion**, hides parts of the bowler — the hand, the arm, the body — to see which body parts carry the useful information.

## What it found

In a 2006 study, Sean Müller, Bruce Abernethy and Damian Farrow tested batters of different skill levels against swing and spin bowlers. They found:

- All batters used some of the same cues.
- **Highly skilled** batters additionally picked up information from **early cues, especially the bowler's hand and arm**, which less skilled batters did not use.

The authors concluded that expertise involves shifting to earlier, more informative sources of information.

## On the field

- **Watching the hand.** Coaches advise watching the bowler's hand at release rather than the whole body, especially against wrist-spinners whose variations look alike.
- **Disguise is a weapon.** Bowlers who release different deliveries from identical hand positions take away this advantage.

## Common misconceptions

- *"Anticipation is guessing."* It is informed prediction based on real cues, refined by thousands of hours of exposure.
- *"You either have it or you don't."* Anticipation skill develops with experience and can be trained.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: why occlusion studies need caution</summary>

Responding to a screen is not the same as batting, where movement and vision are tightly coupled. Later studies with live bowling and occlusion glasses have broadly supported the findings, but effect sizes and the exact timing of useful cues vary between studies.
