## The short answer

A 145 km/h ball takes a little under **half a second** to reach the batter. A simple visual reaction takes around a fifth of a second, and a full stroke takes longer than that to execute. Batters cope by **predicting**. Eye-tracking studies show they jump their gaze ahead of the ball to roughly where it will bounce, then use the bounce to judge the rest of its path.

## What the research found

In a classic 2000 study, Michael Land and Peter McLeod tracked batters' eyes against a bowling machine. They found:

- Batters watched the ball out of the hand, then made a fast **saccade** — a jump of the eyes — to a point near where the ball would bounce. Their eyes arrived there **before** the ball did.
- After the bounce, they tracked the ball briefly.
- The better the batter, the **earlier** the predictive saccade.

In 2013, David Mann and colleagues studied elite international batters facing real bowling. They found the batters kept the **head** tracking the ball very closely, while the eyes again made predictive jumps — including towards where bat and ball would meet.

## Why prediction works

The bounce point carries a lot of information. Once a batter knows where and when the ball lands, its later path is fairly predictable — unless it seams, turns or bounces unevenly. That is exactly why late movement off the pitch is so dangerous: it changes the ball's path after the batter's prediction has been made.

## On the field

- **"Watch the ball" is half true.** Batters watch it out of the hand and after it bounces; in between, their eyes are already waiting.
- **Head position matters.** Keeping the head still and aligned with the ball's line gives the eyes a stable platform.
- **Unfamiliar bowlers feel quicker.** Without a stored model of their release and trajectory, predictions are worse.

## Common misconceptions

- *"Elite batters have faster reactions."* Studies of simple reaction time generally find experts are not much quicker. The advantage is in prediction and reading cues.
- *"You can see the ball onto the bat."* At high speed, the eyes cannot follow the ball continuously to contact.

At 145 km/h, the ball covers about 17.5 m in roughly 0.46 s. If the batter needs about 0.2 s to react and much of the remaining time to move feet and swing the bat, the decision about line and length has to be made early — often around the bounce, or before. Try different speeds in the Flight lab.
