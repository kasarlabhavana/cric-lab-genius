## The short answer

A cricket bat is a carefully shaped piece of **English willow** — light, fibrous and tough enough to survive thousands of impacts. The face is pressed and then knocked in so it does not crack. **Law 5** limits how wide and thick the blade can be, because without limits bats had been getting bigger and more powerful.

## The wood

Professional bats are made from English willow (a cultivated variety of white willow, *Salix alba* 'Caerulea'). It combines low density with good toughness and springiness. Kashmir willow is used for cheaper bats; it is generally denser and harder, so a bat of the same size is heavier and feels less lively.

Makers grade willow by appearance — straight, evenly spaced grain and few blemishes fetch the top grades. Grain appearance is a rough guide to quality, not a guarantee of performance.

## Pressing and knocking-in

Raw willow is soft. Bat-makers **press** the face with rollers to compress the outer fibres and make them harder. A new bat then needs **knocking-in** — repeated gentle strikes with a mallet or old ball — to compress and knit the surface fibres further so the face can take hard impacts without cracking.

## What the Laws allow

Law 5 sets maximum dimensions:

| Measure | Maximum |
|---|---|
| Overall length | 38 in (96.52 cm) |
| Blade width | 4.25 in (10.8 cm) |
| Depth | 2.64 in (6.7 cm) |
| Edge thickness | 1.56 in (4.0 cm) |

The depth and edge limits were introduced in the 2017 Code of the Laws, after bats had grown much thicker. Bats must pass through an official gauge.

## On the field

- **Pick-up and balance matter as much as weight.** Where the wood sits changes how heavy a bat feels to swing.
- **Thicker edges mean edges carry further** — which is exactly why the edge limit exists.

## Common misconceptions

- *"More grains always means a better bat."* Grain count is mostly about appearance and the age of the tree.
- *"Bat size has never been limited."* Width was limited back in the 18th century; depth and edge limits arrived in 2017.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: why the limits matter for physics</summary>

A thicker blade is stiffer and puts more mass behind the impact point, so off-centre hits lose less speed. Big edges turn would-be catches into boundaries. Limiting depth and edges was meant to restore some balance between bat and ball.
