## The short answer

A hit ball is a projectile slowed by air. Without air, 45° would give the longest carry. With drag, high, hanging hits lose a lot of distance, so the best angle is lower — around 39–42° in a drag-only model, and lower still if backspin adds lift. But angle is the small lever. **Exit speed** off the bat is the big one.

## How it works

Two things decide how far the ball carries: how fast it leaves the bat and at what angle.

- **Angle.** Too flat and the ball comes down quickly. Too steep and it spends its speed climbing. In a vacuum the sweet balance is 45°.
- **Drag.** Air drag grows with the square of speed and acts for the whole flight. A steep hit is in the air longer, so drag takes more from it. That pushes the best angle below 45°. In the Six lab's drag-only model, it is about 42° for a 100 km/h hit and about 40° for 140 km/h.
- **Exit speed.** Distance grows roughly with the square of exit speed in a vacuum, and still steeply with drag. In the lab's model, raising exit speed from 120 to 140 km/h adds about 16 m of carry at the best angle.

## On the field

- **Boundary sizes.** ICC playing conditions cap boundaries at 90 yards (82.29 m) from the centre of the pitch and recommend at least 65 yards (59.43 m). A clean strike at a modest angle clears most of them.
- **Flat sixes.** Batters who hit through the line on a flatter trajectory can clear the rope with less height, giving fielders less time to get under the ball.
- **Mishits go up, not out.** A ball struck off the sweet spot leaves slower. To carry the same distance it would need a better angle — but a mishit usually gets a worse one, which is why top edges balloon.

## Common misconceptions

- *"Aim for 45°."* That is the answer for a world without air. With drag a slightly lower angle carries further — though being a few degrees off costs only a metre or two.
- *"Bigger players hit further because they are stronger."* Exit speed comes from bat speed at the sweet spot, which depends on technique and timing as much as strength.

<summary>Go deeper: what this model leaves out</summary>

The Six lab steps the flight forward with gravity and drag. It ignores lift from backspin, which can help a well-struck hit carry further, and it assumes a level ground and still air. A following wind adds distance; a headwind takes it away. Treat the lab as a guide to the shape of the physics, not a predictor of any particular hit.
