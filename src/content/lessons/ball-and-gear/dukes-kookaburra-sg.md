## The short answer

International Test cricket uses three main makes of ball: **Dukes**, **Kookaburra** and **SG**. They all meet Law 4, but they differ in how the seam is stitched and how the leather is finished. Players report that those differences change how long the ball swings, seams and stays hard.

## Who uses what

- **Dukes** — England, and also used in the West Indies.
- **Kookaburra** — Australia, South Africa, New Zealand and several others, as well as most white-ball internationals.
- **SG** — India.

The home board usually chooses the ball for home Tests.

## How they differ

- **Stitching.** Dukes and SG balls are **hand-stitched**. On a Kookaburra, only the **two inner rows** of the main seam hold the halves together; the four **outer rows** are machine-stitched and mainly add grip. Players commonly describe the Kookaburra's seam as flatter once it has worn.
- **Finish.** Each maker uses its own leather treatment and lacquer, which affects how long the shine lasts and how the surface scuffs.
- **Shape retention.** Players widely report that Dukes balls keep their hardness and seam for longer, which helps swing and seam bowlers deeper into an innings.

## What the evidence is

Most comparisons come from players' experience and match statistics. Those are valuable but tangled up with conditions: Dukes balls are mostly used on English pitches under English skies, SG balls on Indian pitches. Separating the ball's effect from the conditions would need controlled testing across all of them, which is rarely published.

## On the field

- **Touring bowlers adjust.** Seam bowlers often say they need different lengths and plans with an unfamiliar ball.
- **Spinners notice the seam.** A more prominent seam can give spinners more purchase on the surface.

## Common misconceptions

- *"One ball is objectively the best."* Each suits different conditions and styles; preferences vary.
- *"Ball differences are all hype."* Construction really does differ — what's uncertain is how much of the on-field difference comes from it.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: why seam prominence matters</summary>

The seam is what trips the boundary layer for conventional swing, and what grips the pitch for seam movement. A seam that stays proud for longer keeps both effects alive. As the stitching wears flat, the ball behaves more like a smooth sphere in the air and skids more off the pitch.
