## The short answer

Broadcast bowling speeds are measured **close to release**, where the ball is moving fastest. They come from radar or, increasingly, from the same camera-based tracking used for reviews. The ball is noticeably slower by the time it reaches the batter.

## Two methods

- **Radar.** A radar gun sends out radio waves; the moving ball reflects them at a slightly changed frequency. That change — the **Doppler shift** — is proportional to the ball's speed towards or away from the gun.
- **Camera tracking.** Multi-camera tracking locates the ball in 3D many times per second. Speed is distance divided by time between positions, taken just after release.

## What the number means

The quoted speed is a single number near the start of the flight. Because drag grows with the square of speed, a 145 km/h ball loses roughly 15–18 km/h before it reaches the batter, and more at the bounce. Two bowlers with the same release speed can arrive at different speeds if one bowls fuller (less time in the air) or gets more skid off the pitch.

## On the field

- **"Heavy ball."** Batters sometimes say a bowler's ball feels quicker than its speed. Steeper bounce, a skiddier pitch or late movement can all make a delivery feel faster.
- **The fastest recorded delivery** in international cricket is widely cited as Shoaib Akhtar's 161.3 km/h in 2003 — with the caveat that measurement methods have changed since.

## Common misconceptions

- *"The speed is how fast the ball hits the bat."* It is the speed near release.
- *"All speed guns agree."* Different systems, positions and calibrations can give slightly different readings.

<details>
<summary>Go deeper: angle matters for radar</summary>

Radar measures speed along its line of sight. A gun placed behind the bowler's arm sees almost the full speed. One placed at an angle sees only part of it and must be corrected. Camera tracking avoids that problem because it measures position in 3D.
